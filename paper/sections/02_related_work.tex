\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Continuation and curricula.}
Classical continuation methods trace solutions through parameterized problems \citep{allgower1990continuation}. For nonconvex optimization, \citet{mobahi2015gaussian} analyze Gaussian-smoothed objectives, while \citet{hazan2016graduated} identify a class for which graduated optimization converges globally. Spatial filtering inside a network is not equivalent to smoothing its loss in parameter space, so these guarantees do not directly apply here. Related approaches include noising the activations and deforning the network in Mollifying Networks \citep{gulcehre2016mollifying}. Curriculum learning progressively changes which examples contribute most to the loss \citep{bengio2009curriculum}. Our methods keep the examples and labels fixed and change their spatial processing.

\paragraph{Spatial information during training.}
Curriculum by Smoothing (CBS) \citep{sinha2020curriculum} is our direct starting point: Gaussian kernels filter feature maps, and their scale decreases during training. Other approaches rely on change the resolution of the input images during training. For instance, \citet{hoffer2019mixmatch} use different image sizes, while \citet{tan2021efficientnetv2} progressively increase the resolution and adjust the regularization accordingly. SDPoint \citep{kuen2018stochastic} also reduces the resolution of internal feature maps. At each training iteration, it randomly selects a block boundary and a reduction ratio, then applies average pooling. Our procedure instead uses max-pooling at fixed locations and progressively reputs the original resolution. While SDPoint allows the inference cost to be adjusted by choosing a downsampling configuration, our modification is temporary and ends at the original inference architecture.


\paragraph{Frequency and aliasing.}
\citet{rahaman2019spectral} describe a bias towards low-frequency functions during neural-network learning. In this setting, frequency refers to variations of the learned function over its input space, which is different from the spatial frequencies of the feature maps we filter. For these feature maps, reducing resolution and removing high frequencies are also different things, and their order matters. Applying a low-pass filter before subsampling can make predictions more stable under small image changes \citep{zhang2019shift}. The choice of where to insert the filters affects generalization \citep{vasconcelos2021aliasing}. The picture is further complicated by nonlinearities, which can introduce new frequency components and aliasing \citep{michaeli2023aliasfree}. These distinctions matter for our Gaussian interventions: placing a blur after a strided convolution does not undo aliasing that has already occurred in that convolution.


\paragraph{Geometry and sensitivity.}
Our procedures recover the original architecture at the end of training, but follow different optimization paths. To examine the resulting models, we draw on the loss visualizations of \citet{li2018visualizing}, using filter-normalized directions and trajectory projections. These plots require care: reparameterizing a network can change its apparent sharpness without changing its predictions \citep{dinh2017sharp}. We also examine local curvature through Hessian--vector products, which avoid constructing the full Hessian \citep{pearlmutter1994hessian,ghorbani2019hessian}. Alongside these weight-space measurements, input Jacobians describe how network outputs respond to changes in the images. They also appear in generalization bounds based on classification margins \citep{sokolic2017robust}. Here, we use these tools to compare the trained models, but dont claim that the measured differences explain their accuracy gains.
