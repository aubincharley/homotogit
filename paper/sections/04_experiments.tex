\section{Experimental study}
\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Selection and evaluation}
The starting exploratory setting uses CIFAR-10 \citep{krizhevsky2009learning}, ResNet-20 \citep{he2016resnet} with BatchNorm \citep{ioffe2015batch}, and 30 epochs of SGD without augmentation. We use all 50,000 training images and the 10,000 test images. As a final step in the exploration, we compared 16 configurations, each trained with three random seeds. For each seed, all configurations shared the same initial weights and training-example order. This comparison helped us choose the three procedures described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.
. R reaches $\uRes\pm\uResSD\%$ test accuracy and RG $\uComb\pm\uCombSD\%$, against $\uPlain\pm\uPlainSD\%$ for plain. Appendix~\ref{app:exploration} gives the comparisons and failures that led to these choices.

We evaluate each model after the final scheduled epoch, not the epoch with the highest test accuracy. Evaluation uses the original network with the added operations disabled and, for BatchNorm, the saved running statistics. For each experimental setting, we report the mean and sample standard deviation across training seeds. To measure a gain over Plain, we first subtract the Plain result for the same seed, with matching initial weights and training-example order. We then report the mean and standard deviation of these differences. Results from separate experimental campaigns are kept separate.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reports final accuracy for every setting, and Table~\ref{tab:transfer-paired} the within-seed gains over plain. each setting changes the optimizer, activation, normalization, architecture, dataset or data size compared to reference. The transfer studies use three seeds each. The spatial ratios and schedule structure are preserved, with adaptations for map size and training budget. In particular, STL-10 \citep{coates2011analysis} uses $96\times96$ inputs, $48\to72\to96$ resolutionreduction, Gaussian scales multiplied by three, and 60 epochs. SVHN \citep{netzer2011reading} uses a fixed 50,000-image training subset. Two reduced-data settings use the same 5,000 CIFAR-10 training examples with either 60 or 293 epochss. The latter approximately matches the reference's 293 epochs but does not match the number of distinct training examples. The training protocols, their adaptations to each setting, and the detailed results are given in Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}.

R improves mean accuracy in all \tSettings{} settings of the table, with positive paired differences in all \tResPositivePairs{} of the \tResPairs{} seed pairs (Table~\ref{tab:transfer-paired}). The mean gains range from $\tResGainMin$ to $\tResGainMax$ points. This consistency extends beyond the original activation and normalization: it holds with GELU, SiLU, GroupNorm and VGG-11-BN \citep{hendrycks2016gelu,ramachandran2017searching,wu2018group,simonyan2015very}. This is evidence about these fixed recipes, rather than a guarantee for arbitrary architectures or training budgets.

The Gaussian gives a less uniform result. With Adam and AdamW \citep{kingma2015adam,loshchilov2019decoupled}, G's mean gain is slightly negative and varies in sign across seeds. With RAdam \citep{liu2020radam}, it is positive but small. RG is close to R under Adam and AdamW, and below it under RAdam. The adaptive optimizers use learning rates selected on plain, seed 0, by final test accuracy; SGD retains the exploratory rate. Their baseline accuracies already exceed the SGD baseline by about nine points. These rows therefore compare interventions within optimizer recipes, not equally tuned optimizers, and the reused test set limits their confirmatory interpretation.

The combined method does not perform best on every dataset. On STL-10, R and G improve the mean, but RG loses $\tRGStlGain$ points, with a negative difference in every seed. With 5,000 CIFAR-10 examples, RG instead gains $\tRGSubShortGain$ points at 2,400 updates and $\tRGSubLongGain$ at 11,720 updates. The longer budget does not remove this benefit. The opposing STL-10 and CIFAR-10-subset results show that sample count alone does not determine whether the combination helps; input resolution, scale adaptation and data distribution also differ.

\subsection{Training loss, test loss and cost}
The initial observation was a higher final training CE together with better test CE and accuracy. A separate five-seed reference study confirms it on the \emph{full} training set: mean train/test CE is $\tLandPlainTrainCE/\tLandPlainTestCE$ for plain, $\tLandRTrainCE/\tLandRTestCE$ for R, $\tLandGTrainCE/\tLandGTestCE$ for G and $\tLandRGTrainCE/\tLandRGTestCE$ for RG (Table~\ref{tab:landscape-endpoints}). These are different runs from the three-seed selection batch (Appendix~\ref{app:landscape}). This pattern is not universal. With GroupNorm, R lowers the 500-image training-probe CE from $\tGnPlainTrainCE$ to $\tGnRTrainCE$ and test CE from $\tGnPlainTestCE$ to $\tGnRTestCE$. On STL-10 it lowers them from $\tStlPlainTrainCE$ to $\tStlRTrainCE$ and from $\tStlPlainTestCE$ to $\tStlRTestCE$. Thus improved generalization need not come with a worse fit to the training probe.

Smaller maps also do not guarantee a wall-time saving (Table~\ref{tab:cost-main}). In the selection batch, R takes about as long as plain, while G and RG add approximately 23\% and 45\% to total wall time, respectively. The transfer timings show the same practical distinction: R stays close to plain, while filtering usually adds cost. Appendix~\ref{app:cost} reports the measured times for all settings. These shared-GPU measurements include evaluation and checkpoint overhead; they do not establish a hardware-independent speedup. All methods recover the original inference computation, so no added filtering or reduction remains at test time. Energy consumption and peak memory are not measured here.
