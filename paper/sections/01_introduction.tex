\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

An idea to minimize a difficult objective is to start from a simpler approximation and progressively recover the target problem. Continuation methods follow this principle by linking problems through a parameterized family and using the previous solution to initialize the next stage \citep{allgower1990continuation,mobahi2015gaussian}. Graduated optimization provides guarantees under specific geometric assumptions \citep{hazan2016graduated}; constructing useful families for neural-network training remains less straightforward.

Our starting point was the Gaussian filtering of CNN feature maps proposed by \citet{sinha2020curriculum}. The intuition is that the model can first focus on large-scale structures before dealing with finer details and textures. We then viewed the Gaussian kernel more generally as a way of controlling spatial information. This led us to temporary resolution reduction, including reduction inside the network. The question became: how much of the observed benefit comes from Gaussian smoothing, and how much can be obtained by simply processing smaller feature maps early in training?

We used CIFAR-10 as an exploratory setting to compare operators, insertion points and schedules, and to select three procedures: resolution reduction, Gaussian filtering, and their combination. All three finish with the original architecture and the added interventions bypassed. In the final comparison, using ResNet-20 and 30 epochs of SGD without augmentation, internal max-pooling improves test accuracy by $\uGainRes$ percentage points over the baseline; the selected combination reaches a gain of $\uGainComb$ points. These are mean differences over three seeds with matched initial weights and data order. The test set also informed method selection, so these findings describe the exploratory setting rather than an independent validation. Appendix~\ref{app:exploration} reports the comparisons leading to this selection.
