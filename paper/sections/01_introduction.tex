\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
An idea to minimize a difficult objective is to start from a simpler approximation and progressively recover the target problem. Continuation methods follow this principle by linking problems through a parameterized family and using the previous solution to initialize the next stage \citep{allgower1990continuation,mobahi2015gaussian}. Graduated optimization provides guarantees under specific geometric assumptions \citep{hazan2016graduated}. Constructing useful families for neural-network training remains less straightforward.

Our starting point was the Gaussian filtering of CNN feature maps proposed by \citet{sinha2020curriculum}. The intuition is that the model can first focus on large-scale structures before dealing with finer details and textures. We then viewed the Gaussian kernel used in this reference more generally as a way of controlling spatial information. This led us to temporarily reduce the resolution in the internal layers of the networl. The question became: how much of the observed benefit comes from Gaussian smoothing, and how much can be obtained by simply processing smaller feature maps early in training?

We used a ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 to explore operators, insertion points and schedules, and to select three procedures: resolution reduction, Gaussian filtering, and their combination. The exploration is reported separately in Appendix~\ref{app:exploration}. We then kept these procedures fixed, with the adaptations required by the new settings, and evaluated their transfer on other datasets, other architectures and optimizers. Resolution reduction gives the most consistent accuracy gains, while the usefulness of the Gaussian depends on the optimizer and dataset. Finally, we examine the trained models through weight perturbations, Hessian calculations and input derivatives. These measurements narrow the possible interpretations: improved test performance does not require the same ordering of local sensitivities, and the treatment of BatchNorm changes what a loss-landscape comparison measures.
