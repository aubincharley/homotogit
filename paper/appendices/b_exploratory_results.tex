\section{CIFAR-10 exploration}
\label{app:exploration}
We used CIFAR-10 to explore interventions, to understand the first results and to select three methods for further study. This exploration was not one homogeneous benchmark. Early pilots used image subsets, a validation split, GroupNorm or a ResNet-18, and shorter training, and their numbers are not comparable with those of the reference protocol. The comparisons that support the final selection use the ResNet-20 reference protocol of Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}, and the selection itself rests on one paired batch. Because we used the test set to choose among methods, these results are exploratory evidence, not an independent validation of the selected methods. The conventions of Appendix~\ref{app:conventions} apply throughout.

We call an arm \emph{resolution-only} when it contains no additional Gaussian continuation. Some resizing operators include their own smoothing, such as antialiased bilinear resizing or MaxBlur; we treat this smoothing as part of the operator.

\subsection{Gaussian filtering as the starting point}
\label{app:explore-gaussian}
\paragraph{Question.} Can training be made easier, or the final network better, by first removing fine spatial detail and then progressively restoring it?

\paragraph{Comparisons and observations.} We first filtered the input images, with a ResNet-20 using GroupNorm, 45,000 training and 5,000 validation images, and 14,040 updates. A fixed blur lowered validation accuracy ($\xInSigHalf\%$ for $\sigma=0.5$ and $\xInSigOne\%$ for $\sigma=1$, against $\xInSigZero\%$ without blur, three seeds). Warm starts that blurred the images during the first 1,500 updates and then removed the blur, in one or two steps, did not recover the unfiltered result ($\xWarmW\%$ and $\xWarmP\%$).

We then moved the filter to the feature maps, following \citet{sinha2020curriculum}: a Gaussian at the 19 convolution outputs, annealed to zero. On a 10,000-image subset with GroupNorm, this lowered validation accuracy ($\xGnGauss\pm\xGnGaussSD\%$ against $\xGnPlain\pm\xGnPlainSD\%$, three seeds). With BatchNorm, two one-seed pilots on the same subset gave the opposite sign: $\xRleGauss\%$ against $\xRlePlain\%$ for a ResNet-18, and $\xRtwGauss\%$ against $\xRtwPlain\%$ for a ResNet-20. Normalization, architecture and initialization changed together, so these pilots do not isolate the cause of the change. Under the reference protocol, the annealed Gaussian reached $\xFdPlateau\pm\xFdPlateauSD\%$ with plateaus and $\xFdGeo\pm\xFdGeoSD\%$ with a geometric decay, against $\xFdPlain\pm\xFdPlainSD\%$ for plain.

\paragraph{Decision.} We continued with feature-map filtering, the ResNet-20 with BatchNorm, the reference protocol and the plateau schedule.

\subsection{From filtering to resolution reduction}
\label{app:explore-resolution}
\paragraph{Question.} If the Gaussian kernel is one way of controlling the information processed early in training, can we obtain a similar effect by temporarily processing smaller representations?

\paragraph{Comparisons and observations.} In a one-seed pilot under the reference protocol, we resized the input images with antialiased bilinear interpolation to $16\times16$ for epochs 0--5 and $24\times24$ for epochs 6--11, then used the original images. With the same seed, this reached $\xProg\%$, against $\xFdPlainSeedZero\%$ for plain and $\xFdPlateauSeedZero\%$ for the Gaussian plateaus. Adding the Gaussian, with its scale multiplied by $r/32$, gave $\xProgGauss\%$. A grid of 21 configurations with three seeds then varied the reduction (input image or stem output, bilinear or max-pooling, three schedules), alone or with the Gaussian at the convolution outputs. The four arms with the $16\to24\to32$ schedule scored between $\xCgStemBil\%$ and $\xCgStemMax\%$, above the Gaussian alone ($\xCgGauss\pm\xCgGaussSD\%$) and plain ($\xCgPlain\pm\xCgPlainSD\%$). Combining input reduction with the Gaussian reached $\xCgInBilGauss\pm\xCgInBilGaussSD\%$. Restricting the Gaussian to the first seven layers ended close to plain ($\xCgEarly\%$).

\paragraph{Decision.} Resolution reduction alone was competitive with the Gaussian in these runs. We therefore studied where in the network to reduce, and with which operator, without any Gaussian.

\subsection{Operators, locations and schedules}
\label{app:explore-operators}
\paragraph{Question.} Where should the reduction be applied, does a more sophisticated operator help, and does the schedule matter?

\paragraph{Comparisons.} A first ablation batch, run with its own initial weights, applied max-pooling with the $16\to24\to32$ schedule after residual blocks 0, 1 and 2 ($\xAbDZero\pm\xAbDZeroSD$, $\xAbDOne\pm\xAbDOneSD$ and $\xAbDTwo\pm\xAbDTwoSD\%$), above its one-seed arms reducing the input image ($\xAbInput\%$) or the stem output ($\xAbStem\%$). We then ran a resolution-only batch of $\rbNConfigs$ targeted configurations with three seeds each, not a full factorial design: seven operators at the input and after block 1; max-pooling at five locations; four schedules for max-pooling, MaxBlur and the $\dot H^{-1}$ reconstruction after block 1; a plain arm and two fixed-resolution controls. Of its $\rbNRuns$ runs, $\rbNValid$ were numerically valid; the perceptual operator after block 1 produced a non-finite loss in all three seeds.

\caption{Resolution-only batch, schedule $16\to24\to32$, three seeds per arm. (a) Location of the max-pooling reduction. (b) Reduction operator after block 1; the perceptual operator diverged in all seeds. The vertical line is the plain mean of this batch.}

\paragraph{Observations.} For max-pooling, the reduction after block 1 had the highest mean ($\rbMaxBOne\pm\rbMaxBOneSD\%$), ahead of blocks 2 and 0 ($\rbLocDTwo$ and $\rbLocDZero\%$) by less than their seed SD, and above the stem output and the input image (Figure~\ref{fig:resolution}a). After block 1, the six valid operators finished between $\rbOpBOneMin\%$ and $\rbOpBOneMax\%$ with overlapping seed ranges, and the more elaborate operators did not score above max-pooling (Figure~\ref{fig:resolution}b). At the input the order differed: the perceptual operator had the highest mean ($\rbInPerceptual\%$), followed by the $\dot H^{-1}$ reconstruction ($\rbInHminus\%$), with max-pooling at $\rbInMax\%$; we do not read this order as an established ranking. The effect of the schedule depended on the operator. With max-pooling, spending epochs 0--5 at 16 and 6--11 at 24 scored above the later ($\rbMaxLate\%$), shorter ($\rbMaxGentle\%$) and reversed ($\rbMaxReverse\%$) schedules, and the same held for the $\dot H^{-1}$ reconstruction, whereas for MaxBlur the reversed order scored marginally higher ($\rbMaxBlurReverse\%$ against $\rbMaxBlurProg\%$). Keeping a fixed $24\times24$ or $16\times16$ reduction until the end gave $\rbFixedTwentyFour\%$ and $\rbFixedSixteen\%$, but these controls are evaluated with a reduced network, so they do not isolate the effect of restoring the resolution.

\paragraph{Decision.} We kept adaptive max-pooling after block 1 with the $16\to24\to32$ schedule: the simplest operator, at the location with the highest mean in both batches.

\subsection{Final comparison and selection}
\label{app:explore-final}
\paragraph{Question.} Which Gaussian placement should we retain, alone and together with the reduction, when all candidates run under identical conditions?

\paragraph{Comparison.} The ablation batch had suggested that the placement matters: filtering the ten post-ReLU sites gave $\xAbPostBlock\pm\xAbPostBlockSD\%$, against $\xAbConv\%$ at the convolution outputs (one seed), and the reduction after block 2 combined with post-ReLU filtering reached $\xAbDTwoG\pm\xAbDTwoGSD\%$. We then ran one paired batch of 16 configurations with three seeds each, evaluated after every epoch. It contained plain, the two Gaussian placements, the reductions of the previous step, these reductions combined with post-ReLU filtering, with or without per-site scale profiles, and the reduction after block 1 combined with filtering at the convolution outputs.

Reduction arms use max-pooling unless labelled; ``profile'' arms rescale the post-ReLU $\sigma$ per site. (b) Difference between each run and the plain run with the same seed.}
\label{fig:unified}

\paragraph{Observations.} Resolution reduction after block 1 improved accuracy by $\uGainRes$ points over plain (Table~\ref{tab:unified-reference}). Without reduction, post-ReLU filtering scored above filtering at the convolution outputs ($\uGauss\%$ against $\uBlurConv\%$, paired difference $\uGaussMinusBlurConv\pm\uGaussMinusBlurConvSD$ points). With the reduction after block 1, the order was reversed: convolution-output filtering reached $\uComb\%$ against $\uResRelu\%$ for post-ReLU filtering (paired difference $\uCombMinusResRelu\pm\uCombMinusResReluSD$). Both differences are positive in each seed. Per-site scale profiles did not give a consistent additional benefit, and \uNearCombCount{} other reduction-plus-Gaussian arms finished within 0.6 points of the highest mean (Figure~\ref{fig:unified}).

The training records show the observation that motivates Section~\ref{sec:theory} (Figure~\ref{fig:unified-curves}). On the current path, the three methods are above plain from the first epochs. On the training probe, their cross-entropy is first below that of plain and ends above it during the last third of training, while their test cross-entropy keeps decreasing where that of plain levels off. \paragraph{Decision.} We retained one method per family: max-pooling after block 1 as the resolution-only method; the Gaussian at the ten post-ReLU sites, the higher of the two placements without reduction, as the Gaussian-only method; and max-pooling after block 1 with the Gaussian at the 19 convolution outputs, the highest mean of the batch, as the combined method. They are representatives of their families, selected with three seeds on a reused test set, not established optimal settings.
